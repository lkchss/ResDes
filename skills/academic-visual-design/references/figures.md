# Figures

For representation, uncertainty, distribution, paired-data, image or diagram decisions, read [figure design evidence and counterexamples](design-evidence/figures.md). The [original synthetic casebook](design-evidence/casebook/index.html) illustrates task-dependent choices; do not treat its plots as universal journal templates.

Start with the scientific question and the relationship to show. Choose a representation that exposes the relevant comparisons, distribution or uncertainty without implying unsupported precision or continuity. Use an appropriate scale and state transformations; avoid truncation or normalization that distorts the intended comparison. Preserve axes, units, category order, group identity, and the mapping from marks and colors to data. Use redundant encodings when color alone could obscure a distinction.

Choose the encoding from the reader's task. For estimates, use comparable positions and supplied intervals when uncertainty matters. For spread or overlap, compare what a binned, smoothed or observation-level display reveals and hides at the actual group sizes. Small groups can make bins or smoothing influential; individual marks can overplot. Choose and disclose the defensible tradeoff rather than automatically selecting a histogram, violin or dot plot. Never invent observations or missing inferential results. A table can complement a figure when exact values or heterogeneous measures matter.

Use shared scales across panels when the intended comparison depends on magnitude. Separate scales may clarify different quantities, but label them and avoid inviting direct magnitude comparisons. Prefer direct labels when they fit without collisions and make mapping immediate; use a legend when repeated or crowded labels would obscure data. Give equally important evidence comparable panel area, contrast and label weight. Unequal area can reflect different complexity or an explicitly justified primary result; decorative hero treatment must not imply stronger evidence.

Two design decisions in context:

- Three groups shown as solid bars with tiny uncertainty whiskers -> points and intervals on one axis, because the reader needs to compare estimates and their precision rather than filled area. Retain the supplied interval type and endpoints.
- Two panels showing the same outcome on different vertical scales -> a shared scale when cross-panel magnitude is the question; if that hides a meaningful within-panel pattern, add a clearly labelled secondary view instead of silently rescaling.

Build quantitative plots from source data in the user's existing Python, R, or other practicable workflow. Keep the plotting source and underlying unrounded data when available. Separate data operations from styling, and document aggregation, exclusions and transformations. Identify uncertainty explicitly (for example, standard error, standard deviation, confidence or credible interval, and level where applicable); never substitute one type for another. Include sample sizes or denominators where needed to interpret the figure.

For an existing figure, inspect the source and rendered artifact. Match labels, marks, limits, annotations, panels and legend to the evidence. Check extrema after aggregation as well as raw-data limits: a valid horizontal range does not prevent a histogram peak or interval from being clipped vertically. When converting from an image alone, distinguish directly recoverable information from estimates; ask for original data if a precise reconstruction cannot be justified. Choose export dimensions and formats using the destination's applicable guidance, not a generic assumed specification. Check readability at intended size, including grayscale or accessible alternatives where appropriate. Inspect both material and composition after the first render; make a bounded correction pass for consequential defects.
